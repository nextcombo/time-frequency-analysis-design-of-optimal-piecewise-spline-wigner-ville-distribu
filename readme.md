# [Time-Frequency Analysis]Design of Optimal Piecewise Spline Wigner-Ville Distribution for TFD Performance Evaluation and Comparison [with MATLAB Code]

This paper primarily contributes to the Time-Frequency Representation (TFR), which is obtained from the Time-Frequency Distribution (TFD), describing the spectral content of a signal over time [1]. It serves as an appropriate representation for analyzing and processing non-stationary signals whose spectral information changes over time [1]. Compared to methods that only consider time or frequency, TFR has shown superior results in various applications such as anomaly detection and classification [2]-[5], localization [6], [7], frequency estimation [8]-[10], signal modeling and synthesis [11]-[12], and parameter estimation [13]-[15]. The accuracy and resolution of the TFD determine its performance [1]. High accuracy ensures minimal crosstalk (interference) while high resolution enhances the ability to distinguish dense components. Visual assessment and TFR measurement are viable tools for evaluating the performance of the TFD [16]. However, visual assessment is subjective, and TFR measurement cannot define the performance differences between different TFDs. Therefore, we propose a new process for evaluating the performance of the TFD by designing a reference optimal TFD and new measures for precision and resolution.
The objectives of this paper are: (1) to design an optimal TFR suitable for multicomponent non-stationary signals with nonlinear instantaneous frequencies (IF) and instantaneous amplitudes (IA); (2) to compare and evaluate the performance of the TFR using the proposed distribution as a reference.
The main work of this paper is as follows: (1) a Probabilistic Wavelet-Ville Distribution (PW-WVD) was designed; (2) the precision and resolution metrics of the time domain finite difference were derived using the PW-WVD; (3) the performance of various time domain finite difference schemes was evaluated using these metrics; (4) a strategy for selecting time domain finite difference schemes was presented with or without prior information on signal parameters.
Figure 1. Flowchart of TFD Performance Evaluation Using the Optimal PW-WVD. The process steps are summarized as follows: (1) Given a set of parameters, generate multicomponent non-stationary signals z(t), (2) calculate the optimal PW-WVD Lz(t, f), (3) compute the WVD Wz(t, f), (4) optimize the kernel γ(t, f), (5) evaluate the TFD performance in terms of precision and resolution, (6) repeat steps 4-5 for different TFDs, (7) repeat the entire process for different signal parameters, and (8) develop a TFD selection strategy based on performance evaluation.
The method proposed in this paper uses the optimal PW-WVD to evaluate the TFD performance as shown in Figure 1, and is summarized as follows:
1) Given a set of parameters (number of components, IF, IA, and time support), use a standard non-stationary signal model to generate a multicomponent signal z(t).
2) Use segmented LFM (PW-LFM) decomposition of z(t) and calculate its optimal PW-WVD Lz(t, f).
3) Compute the signal WVDWz(t, f).
4) Given a kernel γ(t, f), optimize its parameters by minimizing the difference between Lz(t, f) and the TFD.
5) Use the optimized TFD kernel γopt(t, f), Wz(t, f), and Lz(t, f) to evaluate the TFD performance using the proposed precision α and resolution α.
6) Repeat steps 4-5 for different TFD kernels and aggregate the performance evaluations.
7) Repeat steps 1-6 for different signal parameters and aggregate all performance evaluations.
8) Formulate a TFD selection strategy when prior information about the signal parameters is available or not.

Conclusion 1) CKD is used for non-stationary signals with one or two components: the CKD kernel can reduce to pulse without filtering, such as in single-component LFM signals. Moreover, it provides flexible shape adaptation, directionality, and support for compactness to suppress crosstalk, minimize impact on the automatic term. Therefore, we recommend selecting CKD when analyzing signals with one or two components, regardless of their IF complexity.
2) RGD for Multi-component LFM Signals: The RGD and MDD kernels are directional filters suitable for LFM signals. Despite this, the constant short axis support of the MDD kernel leads to low resolution. Similarly, when analyzing single-component LFM signals, the upper limit of RGD kernel parameters prevents it from reducing to a pulse. However, a closer examination of the results in Table I reveals that when P > 1, the regressiveness of RGD becomes insignificant. Therefore, we recommend selecting RGD for multi-component LFM signal analysis. It is worth noting that when relaxing the constraints on MDD's short axis support, MDD performs better than RGD. However, in this work, we have limited our scope to evaluate traditional MDD.
3) CKD Suitable for Non-stationary Signals with Three Components and Nonlinear IF Rules: The high performance of CKD extends to signals with three components and nonlinear IF rules. By analyzing the results in Table I where P = 3, we observe that CKD produces the most consistent high performance compared to other TFDs. Therefore, we recommend selecting CKD for analyzing signals with three components and nonlinear IF rules.
4) CKD, SPWVD or EMBD, Suitable for Non-stationary Signals with Large Number of Components and Nonlinear IF Rules: In analyzing signals with large numbers of components and IF complexity, the CKD, SPWVD, and EMBD kernels are equivalent in terms of TFD precision and resolution. Note that the results in Figure 7 identify SPWVD as one of the best TFDs among them, and they ignore the EMBD utility when there is no information available about the signal parameters. However, it is clear from Table I that SPWVD and EMBD are equivalent, and their utility is only reasonable when P > 3. Therefore, we recommend selecting CKD, SPWVD, or EMBD for analyzing signals with large numbers of components and nonlinear IF rules.
5) It is not recommended to use Ed, BJD, PWVD, BD, MBD, DGF and MPD: The Ed and BJD kernels are directional filters; they uniformly smooth the WVD in all directions. Additionally, the PWVD, BD, and MBD kernels are unidirectional filters; they smooth the WVD along the time or frequency axis. Due to these reasons, none of the previous kernels can maximize TFD accuracy and resolution. On the other hand, DGF is a time-dependent directional kernel designed to maximize TFD energy concentration. Its over-smoothing reduces resolution. Furthermore, with a line-tapped subdictionary, MPD selects many short-time atoms to match the IF nonlinear regions; therefore, it also reduces resolution. For these reasons, aside from their computational complexity, it is not recommended to use DGF and MPD.
